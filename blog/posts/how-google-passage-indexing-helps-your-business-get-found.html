How Google's Passage Indexing Helps Your Business Get Found

October 2026 · 8 min read

Written by

Sabine

Most small business owners think about Google rankings at the page level: either your page ranks for a keyword, or it doesn't. But Google has been quietly doing something more interesting for a while now — it can read a single paragraph buried in the middle of your page, decide that paragraph is the best answer to a specific search query, and rank that passage in search results. Even if the rest of your page isn't particularly relevant to that query.

This is called passage indexing, and it's one of the more under-discussed shifts in how search works. For small business owners in South Africa — especially those who do their own writing or rely on a small team — understanding it can make a real difference to how much organic traffic your website gets, without spending a cent on ads.

What is passage indexing, exactly?

Passage indexing (Google officially calls it "passage ranking") is a feature that lets Google evaluate and rank individual sections of a page independently of the page as a whole. Before this existed, Google ranked pages holistically — the whole page was assessed for relevance to a query. If a page was mainly about one topic, it would generally only rank for searches related to that topic.

With passage indexing, a single section of a long page can appear in search results for a query that the rest of the page doesn't address at all. Google essentially slices your content into logical chunks — often aligned with your headings — and indexes each chunk as a unit. Each passage competes independently in search results.

A practical example: imagine you run a Pretoria-based print shop and you've written a detailed page about your print services. Buried in that page is a paragraph explaining the difference between CMYK and RGB colour modes. Someone in Johannesburg searches "what is the difference between CMYK and RGB". Google might surface that specific paragraph from your print services page in their results — even though your page as a whole is about print services, not colour theory.

How is this different from normal Google search?

In normal search, Google matches a page to a query based on the overall relevance of the page. The title, headings, meta description, and general content all contribute to how Google understands what the page is about. A page about print design would rank for print design queries. It wouldn't typically rank for colour theory queries, even if colour theory is mentioned somewhere on the page.

Passage indexing changes that. It means each section of your page has its own ranking potential. A long, well-structured page can rank for many different queries — not just the one the page is "about". That's a significant opportunity, especially for pages that cover a topic in depth.

It also means the opposite is true: if your content is dense, unstructured, and hard to read, Google has a harder time identifying where one idea ends and another begins. Those pages are less likely to benefit from passage indexing, because Google can't clearly isolate the passages.

Why does it matter for small business websites?

For large brands with hundreds of pages and dedicated SEO teams, passage indexing is helpful but not transformational. They already rank for a lot of queries. For small businesses, it's potentially a much bigger deal.

Here's why: most small business websites don't have many pages. You have a homepage, a few service pages, maybe an about page, and a handful of blog posts. You're competing for a limited number of search rankings across a small amount of content. Passage indexing means each of those pages can now rank for far more than one keyword — as long as the content on the page is detailed enough and structured clearly enough for Google to extract distinct passages from it.

A single well-written service page — say, a brand strategy page — might cover what brand strategy is, why it matters, what's included in a brand strategy package, how long it takes, and what questions to ask when choosing a designer. Each of those sections, properly written and headlined, can rank independently for its own set of search queries.

That's potentially five, six, seven different search queries your one page can appear for — without you writing any additional content. You just need to write the one page well.

What kind of content benefits most from passage indexing?

Not all content benefits equally. Passage indexing is most powerful on longer pages — typically 1,000 words or more — that cover a topic from multiple angles. Short pages with a single focus don't give Google many distinct passages to work with. Long, rambling pages without clear structure also struggle, because Google can't easily isolate where one idea ends and the next begins.

The sweet spot is content that:

  • Covers a topic in genuine depth (not padded with filler)
  • Uses clear subheadings (H2 and H3 tags) to signal topic shifts
  • Answers specific questions within individual sections
  • Is written in plain, readable language — not keyword-stuffed
  • Has each section able to stand alone and make sense out of context

That last point is important. Google needs to be able to show a passage to someone who hasn't read the rest of your page. If a section only makes sense in the context of what came before it ("As we mentioned above..."), it's less useful as a standalone passage. Write each section so it works on its own.

How to structure your pages to benefit from passage ranking

The good news is that the structural changes needed to benefit from passage indexing are exactly the same changes that make your content better for humans. This isn't about gaming the algorithm — it's about writing clearly.

Use descriptive H2 headings that phrase the topic as a question or statement. "What does a brand strategy package include?" is a more useful heading than "Package details". The first one mirrors how someone might actually search. Google can recognise it as a distinct passage about package inclusions. The second one is vague and could mean almost anything.

Give each section enough substance to be genuinely useful on its own. Two sentences under a heading is rarely enough. If your section heading promises to explain something, explain it — in enough detail that someone could read just that section and leave with a useful answer.

Avoid over-relying on bullet lists for everything. Lists are useful, but a page made entirely of short bullet points doesn't give Google much prose to parse for meaning. Mix lists with proper paragraphs that explain context and nuance.

Cover the questions people actually ask, not just the points you want to make. Before writing a service page or blog post, think about what questions your ideal client types into Google. Write a section that directly answers each one. Tools like Google's "People also ask" box are a good source — just search for your main topic and see what related questions come up.

Don't bury your best content. If you have a section that directly answers a common question, lead with the answer, then add context. Don't make the reader — or Google — wade through three paragraphs of context before getting to the point. This is good writing practice generally, but it's especially important when your passage might be surfaced in isolation.

Does passage indexing only apply to blog posts?

No — and this is a common misconception. Passage indexing applies to any page on your website: service pages, about pages, FAQ pages, product descriptions. Blog posts are the obvious place to write long-form content, but your service pages shouldn't be thin either.

A service page that only says "we design logos and brand identities, contact us for a quote" is leaving a lot of search traffic on the table. A service page that explains what a logo design process involves, what the difference is between a logomark and a wordmark, how long it typically takes, what files you'll receive at the end, and what to look for in a brand identity designer — that page has multiple passages that can independently rank for multiple queries.

Your about page is another underused opportunity. If it just says "we're a design studio based in Pretoria", it's a dead end for search traffic. If it tells the story of how the studio was founded, describes the team's expertise, explains the kinds of businesses you typically work with, and answers the question "why should I choose Cat's Creations over another designer?" — now you have a page with real passage potential.

Passage indexing and AI-powered search

As Google integrates more AI into its search results — including AI Overviews that summarise answers at the top of search results pages — passage indexing becomes even more relevant. AI Overviews pull from specific sections of pages, not from pages as a whole. They're essentially a more visible, more prominent version of the same passage-surfacing logic.

If your content is well-structured with clear sections that directly answer questions, you're much more likely to be cited in an AI Overview. This is the same principle that makes content rank for Featured Snippets — the boxed answers that appear at the top of search results. Clear, direct answers to specific questions, written in a way that makes sense out of context, are what get surfaced.

For a local business like a brand strategy studio in Pretoria, showing up in an AI Overview for a question like "what does brand strategy include" or "how long does a logo design take" could send qualified traffic to your site even without a traditional first-page ranking.

A quick checklist: is your content passage-friendly?

Before you publish your next page or blog post, ask yourself:

  • Does each H2 heading clearly describe the specific topic of that section?
  • Could someone read just one section and walk away with a useful, complete answer?
  • Is the page at least 800–1,000 words, covering the topic from multiple angles?
  • Have you addressed the questions your audience actually searches for — not just the points you wanted to make?
  • Are your best answers near the top of each section, not buried at the end?
  • Is the writing clear and readable, without keyword-stuffing or unnecessary jargon?

If you can check all of those boxes, your content is in a strong position to benefit from passage indexing — and from the broader shift towards AI-powered search that's happening right now.

The bottom line for South African small businesses: you don't need a massive website or a big SEO budget to benefit from how Google ranks content today. You need fewer, better-written pages — structured clearly, covering topics in real depth, answering the questions your customers are actually typing into search. That's always been good writing practice. Now it's also how Google finds and surfaces your best work.
